Classification

According to UNESCO's classification system, languages can be categorized into one of six levels of endangerment. The most important factor in determining a language's status is whether or not it is passed down from one generation to the next.

Category Description Examples
Safe The language is spoken by all generations and passed down without issue. English, Hindi, Indonesian, Spanish
Vulnerable Most children speak the language, but only at home or in other specific contexts. Basque, Belarusian, Gondi, Welsh
Definitely Endangered Children no longer learn the language natively. Corsican, Kangri, Romani, Yiddish
Severely Endangered The language is spoken by grandparents and older, and it is understandable to parents. Breton, Mapuche, Nafusi, Picard
Critically Endangered The language is spoken exclusively by grandparents and older, though it is rarely used. Ainu, Duruwa, Mayo, Sirmauri
Extinct The language has no more living native speakers. Egyptian, Dalmatian, Hupa, Itonama

Why Does It Happen?

If keeping a language alive is seemingly as simple as passing it on to the next generation, you may be wondering how any language could even begin to die out. As it turns out, there are a variety of factors that may contribute to the endangerment or even extinction of a language.

Extermination

Speakers of minority languages may be put into danger through colonialism, genocide, natural disasters, or disease.

Marginalization

In order to earn money or achieve a higher social status, marginalized individuals may be pressured to abandon their native language.

Repression

Governments may attempt to promote a single national culture, limiting the presence of minority languages in a given society.
